About MoonWindow

Landers near the Moon's south pole live on a knife edge. The Sun and Earth both hang near the horizon there, so a small change of landing spot or date can switch solar power or direct radio contact on or off. MoonWindow shows that at a glance for any site and date.

What you see

  • Sky view: a 360° picture of the sky from the landing site. Left to right is compass direction, up and down is height above the horizon. The sky is black because the Moon has no air, even in daylight. Dotted tracks show where the Sun and Earth move over the next 30 days.
  • Sunlight and Earth cards: whether each is above the horizon now, how high, and how long until it sets or rises.
  • 30-day timeline: when there is sunlight, when Earth is in view, and when you have both. Drag or tap to choose a time, or focus the timeline and use the arrow keys to step by one hour.
  • Mission: compare power and visibility for up to three scenarios using shared solar settings. Each timeline has a landing-date finder below it.

How it's calculated

For each moment, the app gets the positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon from the Astronomy Engine library, turns the Moon to the orientation given by the IAU/WGCCRE 2009 model (the same mean Earth/polar axis frame that LRO, LOLA and LROC coordinates use), and works out the direction to the Sun and Earth from the landing site. Elevation is the angle above the ideal flat horizon, and azimuth is measured from north toward east. Whether the Sun or Earth is actually visible then depends on the skyline in that direction. Timelines use one-hour steps, and countdowns are refined to the minute.

Real terrain from NASA LOLA

At the poles, mountains and crater rims decide whether the low Sun and Earth are visible. For each south polar site, the app uses a skyline computed from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) south polar elevation models: an 80 m grid from 80°S to the pole and a 240 m grid beyond it (LDEM_80S_80MPP_ADJ and LDEM_60S_240MPP_ADJ; Barker et al. 2023, NASA Goddard Planetary Geodesy Data Archive). For every half degree of compass direction, we trace a line outward over the curved Moon for 400 km and record the highest terrain angle seen from 2 m above the ground. This is the same approach NASA's polar illumination maps used (Mazarico et al. 2011). Use "Terrain" at the top to compare with flat ground.

Accuracy and limits

Each scenario links to the same query in NASA JPL Horizons so you can compare the numbers.

  • Terrain detail. The 80 m and 240 m grids capture mountains, ridges and crater rims but not boulders or small craters near the lander. At the pole, a few hundred metres matter: on the connecting ridge, two points 200 m apart get 88% and 78% sunlight over a year. Listed south polar sites use precomputed 80 m skylines. Any other point south of 77°S, including custom coordinates, gets a skyline computed in your browser from 240 m LOLA terrain (960 m for distant mountains). That is accurate on plains and plateaus and approximate on narrow ridges. Points north of 77°S use flat ground.
  • Visibility thresholds. Earth visibility uses its centre. The Mission panel lets you choose whether any part, the centre, or the entire Sun must clear the horizon. Power estimates use the visible fraction of the solar disk.

Landing sites

Coordinates (latitude, east longitude) from: LROC featured images for IM-1, IM-2, Chang'e 6 and Blue Ghost 1; the USGS Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature for Chandrayaan-3 (Statio Shiv Shakti); NASA NSSDC for Apollo 11 and SLIM. The Shackleton ridge points come from our own LOLA analysis. For each of NASA's 9 Artemis III candidate regions, we searched the LOLA 80 m map within 5 km of the region's published coordinate for the sunniest 100 m spot that meets NASA's landing rules (slope under 10°, Earth in view at least 25% of the year). The region coordinate itself often sits on a steep slope or in shadow.

Checking the slope method: NASA estimated a 12° slope where IM-1 Odysseus came to rest and tipped over. Our LOLA slope there is 11.6°.

Words to know

Elevation
Height above the horizon in degrees. 0° is on the horizon, 90° is straight overhead, negative is below.
Azimuth
Compass direction: 0° north, 90° east, 180° south, 270° west.
Lunar day
One full day and night on the Moon lasts about 29.5 Earth days.
Libration
The Moon's slow wobble as seen from Earth. From the Moon, it makes Earth trace a small loop in the sky about once a month.
Direct-to-Earth (DTE)
Radio contact with Earth without a relay satellite. Possible only when Earth is above the local horizon.
Permanently shadowed region
Crater floors near the poles that never get sunlight, where water ice can survive.
