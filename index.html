About MoonWindow

Landers near the Moon's south pole live on a knife edge. The Sun and Earth both hang near the horizon there, so a small change of landing spot or date can switch solar power or direct radio contact on or off. MoonWindow shows that at a glance for any site and date.

What you see

  • Sky view: a 360° picture of the sky from the landing site. Left to right is compass direction, up and down is height above the horizon. The sky is black because the Moon has no air, even in daylight. Dotted tracks show where the Sun and Earth move over the next 30 days.
  • Sunlight and Earth cards: whether each is above the horizon now, how high, and how long until it sets or rises.
  • 30-day timeline: when there is sunlight, when Earth is in view, and when you have both. Click it to jump to that moment.
  • Comparison: add up to three scenarios, for example the same site a week later after a launch slip.

How it's calculated

For each moment, the app gets the positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon from the Astronomy Engine library, turns the Moon to the orientation given by the IAU/WGCCRE 2009 model (the same mean Earth/polar axis frame that LRO, LOLA and LROC coordinates use), and works out the direction to the Sun and Earth from the landing site. Elevation is the angle above the ideal flat horizon, and azimuth is measured from north toward east. Whether the Sun or Earth is actually visible then depends on the skyline in that direction. Timelines use one-hour steps, and countdowns are refined to the minute.

Real terrain from NASA LOLA

At the poles, mountains and crater rims decide whether the low Sun and Earth are visible. For each south polar site, the app uses a skyline computed from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) south polar elevation model (LDEM_60S_240MPP_ADJ, 240 m grid; Barker et al. 2023, NASA Goddard Planetary Geodesy Data Archive). For every half degree of compass direction, we trace a line outward over the curved Moon for 400 km and record the highest terrain angle seen from 2 m above the ground. This is the same approach NASA's polar illumination maps used (Mazarico et al. 2011). Use "Terrain" at the top to compare with flat ground.

Accuracy and limits

Each scenario links to the same query in NASA JPL Horizons so you can compare the numbers.

  • Terrain detail. The 240 m grid captures mountains and crater rims but not boulders or small craters near the lander. Sites outside the south polar map, and custom coordinates, use flat ground.
  • Centers, not edges. "Up" means the center of the Sun or Earth is above the horizon. The Sun's disk is about half a degree wide, so near the poles it can be partly visible.

Words to know

Elevation
Height above the horizon in degrees. 0° is on the horizon, 90° is straight overhead, negative is below.
Azimuth
Compass direction: 0° north, 90° east, 180° south, 270° west.
Lunar day
One full day and night on the Moon lasts about 29.5 Earth days.
Libration
The Moon's slow wobble as seen from Earth. From the Moon, it makes Earth trace a small loop in the sky about once a month.
Direct-to-Earth (DTE)
Radio contact with Earth without a relay satellite. Possible only when Earth is above the local horizon.
Permanently shadowed region
Crater floors near the poles that never get sunlight, where water ice can survive.
